Your prompt cache has a heartbeat, and naive LLM routers stop it

23 September 2026

There's a claim doing the rounds that LLM routers are dangerous on long coding sessions because switching models blows away your prompt cache. I built a router, believed that, and then did the arithmetic. The claim is wrong as usually stated, and the real mechanism is both stranger and more useful.

The setup

Coding agents accumulate enormous context. An hour into a session with Goose or Cursor you're carrying 60,000 tokens of repository maps, diffs and terminal output, and every turn re-sends the lot. This is why prompt caching exists. A frontier model charging $10 per million input tokens charges around $1 for cached ones. DeepSeek charges $0.07 against $0.55.

So when a router sees a trivial follow-up — what line is that function on? — and sends it to a cheap flash model, what actually happens?

The naive calculation says switching wins

Sixty thousand tokens on a cached anchor at $1/M is $0.06. The same context on a flash model at $0.075/M uncached is $0.0045. Switching is thirteen times cheaper.

And the usual explanation for why it isn't — that the detour evicts the anchor's cache — doesn't hold either. Provider caches are keyed on the prompt prefix and expire on a timer. Sending one request somewhere else doesn't reach into the other provider and delete anything.

If that were the whole picture, routers would be free money. They aren't, and the reason is more specific.

The cache runs on a heartbeat

The detail that makes this work is that each cache hit resets the clock. A provider prompt cache has a time-to-live — typically five minutes of inactivity — and every turn you send to the anchor refreshes it.

In an active session, turns arrive every two or three minutes. So routing them to the anchor isn't just using the cache, it's the thing keeping the cache alive. It's a heartbeat.

Divert an intermediate turn to a flash model and you remove one beat. The gap between anchor hits widens past the TTL, the cache lapses, and the next anchor turn has to rewrite the entire context. On Anthropic, cache writes carry a 1.25× surcharge over base input, so rebuilding 60,000 tokens on a $10/M tier costs $0.75.

That's the whole mechanism. The detour doesn't evict anything. It removes the heartbeat that was keeping the cache alive, and the timer does the rest.

The last row is the one that matters. A detour taken to save five and a half pence ends up costing twelve and a half times what staying put would have cost.

Two other ways switching loses

The alternative isn't always cheap

Flash models are an order of magnitude below the anchor's cached rate, so they win on the single turn. Mid-tier models often aren't. Routing those 60,000 tokens to mistralai/mistral-medium-3-5 at $1.50/M uncached costs $0.090 against $0.060 to stay — 50% more, to use a weaker model, with no TTL lapse required. Anything priced above the anchor's cached rate loses on contact once the context is large.

Wrong answers cost whole turns

If the cheap model misreads the context and you have to re-ask, you don't just pay for the retry. On a coding agent a bad edit tends to cost two or three follow-up turns to notice and undo, each carrying the full context again. A saving measured in thousandths of a dollar is wiped out by a single misroute, and the frequency of misroutes is a property of your classifier, not your price list.

The rule that falls out of this

Switch away from a warm anchor only when the expected cost of doing so beats the cost of staying:

Expected switch cost
  = cost(candidate, uncached)
  + P(rebuild) × cost(anchor, rebuild)

Switch only if that is < cost(anchor, cached)

The interesting term is P(rebuild), the probability that the anchor cache lapses before you next need it. It isn't a constant, and it isn't a context-length threshold. It's a function of elapsed idle time and the expected interval to the next anchor turn, measured against the TTL. Three cases fall straight out of it:

  • Mid-session, turns arriving steadily. P(rebuild) approaches 1, so almost nothing justifies the detour. Staying anchored is 70–90% cheaper.
  • Early in a thread, under about 4,000 tokens. No cache exists yet, so there's nothing to lapse. P(rebuild) is 0 and cheap routes are close to free money.
  • Terminal turns. A standalone tool call or an end-of-session summary, where no later turn will want the anchor context. P(rebuild) is 0 again.

My first implementation used a flat 12,000-token cut-off instead: above that, stay anchored. It's a crude proxy for a sum you can just do properly, and it gets both ends wrong — too sticky on terminal turns, not sticky enough during a fast editing loop.

That distinction matters, so I've kept the full derivation of all three in the repository's README rather than asking anyone to take $64.80 on trust.

The naive column is the interesting one. It routes more traffic to cheap tiers than my router does — 40% to flash against my 50%, but far more of it deep inside long threads — and still spends nearly twice as much, because $14.20 of it goes on rebuilding caches it kept letting lapse.

One number I didn't expect: my flash tier handled 920 requests but only 0.70M uncached input tokens, about 760 tokens per request. The cheap routes are nearly all happening early in threads, before caching is in play. That's the break-even rule doing exactly what it should, and it's the clearest evidence in the data that the mechanism is real rather than a story fitted after the fact.

14 turns still needed re-asking on a stronger model, and 41 ambiguous turns were elevated by the confidence gate rather than being sent down a tier. Those two figures are the honest cost of the approach.

A note on the classifier

The routing decision itself has to come from somewhere, and I used TypeSafe's Jev because it returns calibrated probabilities rather than a label. The retry term in the rule above is weighted by confidence, so a classifier that only says flash without saying how sure it is can't feed the calculation.

What I'd weigh differently now is latency rather than price. A hosted classifier sits in the request path, so its round trip is added to every turn — and third-party measurements put Jev nearer 240–370ms than the bottom of its stated 70–500ms range. Local typed-decision models run an order of magnitude faster, around 30ms on a laptop, because there's no network hop at all.

The catch is that the small local models are weak zero-shot on this kind of task and need fine-tuning on your own distribution to be competitive, and they ship overconfident until their temperature is refit — which breaks the confidence gate until you fix it. For a narrow, repeated decision like tier selection, with a few hundred logged examples to fine-tune on, I'd expect a local model to win. That's the next thing I want to test rather than something I've measured.

Where this leaves routing

The useful framing isn't route every prompt to the cheapest model that can handle it. It's know when not to switch. Cheap routes at the start of a thread are nearly free money. The same route twenty turns in is a bet on two things — that you'll be back at the anchor before the timer runs out, and that the cheap model won't make you redo the work — at odds you can calculate before you place it.

The implementation is at github.com/robinwintertaylor/Prompt-Router. It uses TypeSafe's Jev for classification today, falls back to local heuristics when that isn't available, and I'd take a pluggable local model over both. MIT licensed, and I'd welcome people telling me where the maths is wrong.
