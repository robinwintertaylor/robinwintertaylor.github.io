When switching models mid-session costs more than staying

Robin Winter-Taylor, 23 September 2026

There's a claim doing the rounds that LLM routers are dangerous on long coding sessions because switching models blows away your prompt cache. I built a router, believed that, and then did the arithmetic. It's more interesting than that, and the real rule is more useful.

The setup

Coding agents accumulate enormous context. An hour into a session with Goose or Cursor you're carrying 60,000 tokens of file trees, diffs and terminal output, and every turn re-sends the lot. This is why prompt caching exists: Anthropic charges roughly a tenth for cached input tokens, DeepSeek about an eighth.

So when a router sees a trivial follow-up — what line is that function on? — and sends it to a cheap flash model, what actually happens?

The naive calculation says switching wins

Sixty thousand tokens on a cached frontier anchor at $1/M works out at $0.06. The same context on a flash model at $0.075/M uncached is $0.0045. Switching is thirteen times cheaper.

So the cache-thrashing story, told as switching always costs more, is simply wrong at these numbers. If that were the whole picture, routers would be free money and there would be nothing to write about.

Three things flip it

The alternative isn't always cheap

Route to another frontier model rather than a flash model and you pay $10/M uncached on 60,000 tokens, which is $0.60, against $0.06 to stay put. Ten times worse. Frontier-to-frontier switching is where a naive router genuinely burns money, and it's the case the original warning was really about.

The cache has a clock

Your anchor's cache doesn't die because you went elsewhere. It dies because nobody touched it before the time-to-live expired. Take a two-minute detour and come back and it's still warm. Take a fifteen-minute one and the next anchor turn costs a full uncached ingest: $0.60 on the numbers above. A saving of $0.0045 has turned into a cost of $0.60, more than a hundred times worse. The variable isn't the switch, it's how long you stay away.

Wrong answers cost a whole turn

This is the big one. If the cheap model misreads the context and you re-ask on the anchor, the retry costs $0.06 warm or $0.60 cold. Each successful cheap route saves about $0.0555. So if a failed cheap route forces a cold re-ingest, the cheap model has to be right roughly 92% of the time just to break even.

Routing accuracy, not token price, is what decides whether a router saves money. That reframes the whole problem.

The rule that falls out of this

Switch away from a cached anchor only when the expected cost of doing so is lower than the cost of staying:

(N × alt_price)
  + P(TTL expiry)  × (N × anchor_uncached)
  + P(retry)       × retry_cost

  <  N × anchor_cached

Everything in there is knowable at routing time: the context length, the price of both models, how long the detour is likely to last, and the classifier's confidence in its own decision. It's a computation, not a threshold.

Which is what my first implementation got wrong. I used a flat 12,000-token cut-off, above which sessions stayed anchored. That's a crude proxy for a sum you can just do properly.

The baseline is the same traffic priced as if every turn had gone to a frontier model, with cached input priced at the cached rate. It's a sample of one developer over one week, so treat it as a shape rather than a benchmark. The export script is in the repo if you want to run the same measurement on your own traffic.

Where this leaves routing

The useful framing isn't route every prompt to the cheapest model that can handle it. It's know when not to switch. Cheap routes on short threads are nearly free money. Cheap routes deep into a cached session are a bet on classifier accuracy, at odds you can calculate before you place it.

The implementation is at github.com/robinwintertaylor/Prompt-Router, built on TypeSafe's Jev for the classification step, because a decision model that returns a confidence score is exactly what the retry term in that formula needs. It's MIT licensed, and I'd welcome people telling me where the maths is wrong.
